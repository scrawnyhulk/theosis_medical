Personal fat threshold: How type 2 diabetes develops

An interactive model of how overflowing fat storage can lead to type 2 diabetes, and how that path can run in reverse.

What does this mean?

Remember that fat and carbohydrates are fuel. If we take in more fuel than we need, we store it in the fuel tanks: fat cells, and glycogen stores in the liver and muscle.

We tend to think of type 2 diabetes as just a “sugar problem,” but how we handle excess fuel matters too. Glycogen is stored sugar in the liver and muscle. These reserves are limited and refill after we eat. With continued excess intake, carbohydrate can be turned into fat, and the body can also store more of the fat we eat. If healthy fat storage becomes strained, fat can accumulate in tissues and interfere with insulin. Blood sugar rises when insulin can no longer keep up and the liver releases too much glucose. Limited healthy fat storage can therefore help drive type 2 diabetes.

People differ in how much fat they can store under the skin without metabolic strain. Genetics help shape that capacity. Some people can keep expanding that tank for a long time. For others, even a modest weight gain can matter.

The slider is a model of that overflow, not your personal threshold. First, watch glycogen replenish in the liver and muscle. Then pinchable fat builds under the skin. Muscle is often the first tissue to respond less to insulin, which sends more incoming fuel to the liver to be made into fat. As healthy storage comes under strain, fat accumulates around the organs and within the liver, then the pancreas, where it can impair the beta cells that make insulin. This order follows Professor Roy Taylor’s twin-cycle hypothesis. In real life these processes overlap; they do not fill one at a time. Full glycogen stores are normal and do not themselves cause diabetes. If insulin can no longer keep up, glucose stays elevated in the blood, often alongside higher circulating fats. That can lead to type 2 diabetes.

This is not type 1. Type 1 usually involves autoimmune destruction of the cells that make insulin, leaving the body unable to make enough insulin. Different disease.

Reversal in real life

In the DiRECT trial, an ~850 kcal/day diet-replacement programme put 46% of people in remission at 12 months and 36% at 24 months (HbA1c below 6.5% off all diabetes medicines).

Best odds: diabetes for under 6 years, losing 15 kg or more (86% remission), and beta cells that recover. Remission is not a cure; keep checking A1c.

From the clinic

How to tell if you may be near your threshold

Blood sugar is usually one of the last things to change. These earlier clues point toward overflowing fat storage and insulin resistance. They are screening clues, not a diagnosis; talk with your clinician about testing.

1Waist-to-height ratio of 0.5 or more

Keep your waist under half your height. NICE classifies 0.5–0.59 as increased and 0.6 or more as high central adiposity, both linked to higher risk of type 2 diabetes. These bands apply to adults with a BMI under 35.

Measure your waist midway between the lowest rib and the top of the hip bone. Calculated on this device only. Nothing is saved or sent.

2Triglyceride-to-HDL ratio of 3 or more

Divide fasting triglycerides by HDL cholesterol (both in mg/dL). In overweight adults, a ratio of 3.0 or more (1.8 in mmol/L), or triglycerides of 130 mg/dL or more, helped identify insulin resistance. These lipid clues are less reliable in African American adults.

3High fasting insulin

The pancreas can keep glucose normal for years by making more insulin, so fasting insulin often rises first. Labs use different assays and there is no universal cut-off; one study used 109 pmol/L or more (about 16 µIU/mL).

4Glucose and A1c change late

Fasting glucose and HbA1c often stay normal until the beta cells can no longer keep up. An A1c of 5.7–6.4% is the prediabetes range; 6.5% or more is the diabetes range.

Lower thresholds in South Asian people

In 490,288 UK Biobank adults, the diabetes prevalence seen at a BMI of 30 in white participants occurred at a BMI of 22 in South Asian participants (26 in Black participants and 24–26 in Chinese participants). The matching waist sizes for South Asian men and women were 79 cm and 70 cm, compared with 102 cm and 88 cm.

Lipodystrophy: the threshold at its most extreme

People with lipodystrophy cannot store fat normally under the skin. Even when they look lean, they often develop diabetes, very high triglycerides and fatty liver. In lipodystrophic mice, transplanting healthy fat reversed the diabetes and sharply lowered insulin levels.

The twin cycle: why fat in the liver and pancreas matters

When fat can no longer be stored safely under the skin, it overflows into the liver and then the pancreas. Two linked vicious cycles follow. Losing that overflow fat reverses both.

Liver and pancreas fat figures are averages from the cited study groups, shown to illustrate the typical pattern. They are not targets or predictions for any one person, and no single number marks an individual’s threshold.

What the studies measured

  • 8 weeks, 600 kcal/day (Counterpoint): fasting glucose was normal within 1 week; liver fat fell from 12.8% to 2.9%, pancreas fat from 8.0% to 6.2%, and the beta cells' early (first-phase) insulin response rose to near-normal levels.1
  • DiRECT, primary care, ~15% weight loss: liver fat fell from 16.0% to 3.1% and pancreas fat fell in everyone who lost the weight.2
  • Beta-cell recovery decides remission: only the people whose first-phase insulin response recovered went back to non-diabetic glucose levels. They had had diabetes for a shorter time (2.7 vs 3.8 years).2
  • Remission rates: 46% at 12 months, 36% at 24 months.3

The organ fat in the figure is illustrative: it shows the direction of change, not your scan. Ask your clinician before starting a very-low-calorie diet, especially if you take insulin or a sulfonylurea.

About the model & sources

About the abdominal views. These are CT-style teaching diagrams, not patient scans or measured segmentations. The muscle wall separates subcutaneous fat from the deep abdominal compartment. Fat is darker than muscle on CT; gold, orange, and purple overlays identify the modeled compartments. The baseline stays fixed and the current view uses the same field of view. The drawing approximates upper-abdominal relationships rather than reproducing an exact scan level. Added visceral fat drives the deeper expansion here. The standing figure’s organs retain their original dimensions, while fat grows around them. Intra-organ fat changes tissue appearance, not organ size. The CT-style rendering uses darker fat, varied soft-tissue shading, irregular bowel loops, renal detail, and cortical and trabecular bone to approximate imaging appearance. Both subcutaneous and visceral fat can increase waist size. Neither body shape nor this diagram determines an individual’s fat distribution or diabetes status. Reference anatomy: Undergraduate Diagnostic Imaging Fundamentals: Abdomen. No patient-scan pixels are used in this procedural teaching view. See NIDDK: Body fat distribution and health risks.

About the order of the stages. The numbered stages follow Professor Roy Taylor’s twin-cycle hypothesis. Muscle insulin resistance comes early: in young, lean, insulin-resistant adults, muscle stored about 60% less glycogen after carbohydrate meals while the liver made more than twice as much new fat. Higher insulin then drives liver fat. A fatty liver resists insulin, raises glucose output and exports more triglyceride, and fat reaching the pancreas impairs beta-cell insulin secretion, at first reversibly. On a 600 kcal/day diet, liver fat fell and fasting glucose normalized within 1 week, and beta-cell function recovered over 8 weeks as pancreas fat fell. The stages still overlap in real life.

About remission. In DiRECT (adults aged 20–65 with type 2 diabetes for under 6 years, BMI 27–45, not on insulin), 46% of the intervention group were in remission at 12 months and 36% at 24 months, compared with 4% and 3% of controls. Remission was defined as HbA1c below 6.5% after at least 2 months off all diabetes medicines. People whose glucose returned to normal had recovered first-phase insulin secretion and had a shorter diabetes duration. In ReTUNE, 14 of 20 people with type 2 diabetes and a BMI under 27 reached remission after losing a median of 6.5% of their weight.

About the rotatable 3D figure. The optional 3D view loads only when you tap 3D, using Theosis Health's own procedurally built anatomy (MakeHuman CC0 body base) and a Three.js renderer. Fat + muscle shows a gradually expanding outer subcutaneous layer over named anatomical muscles. A separate visceral control expands the abdominal wall and the actual omental and mesocolic meshes. Inside abdomen provides a teaching cutaway of the liver, pancreas and bowel. The geometry is anatomical reference material; regional fat deformations, color changes and stage timing remain illustrative rather than patient measurements. Glycogen is retained in the reserve meters and original 2D fallback. This model cannot determine individual organ-fat volume, diabetes risk or remission from body shape.

About glycogen. Glycogen is a reserve of stored glucose in liver and muscle. The liver can use it to support blood glucose; muscle uses its own glycogen for activity. These reserves are limited and change with meals and movement. See NCBI Bookshelf: Glycogenolysis. Carbohydrate can also be converted into fat during sustained excess intake, as examined in a human carbohydrate-overfeeding study. The glycogen-first segment is a teaching sequence, not a requirement that glycogen must be full before any fat can be stored. Its teal highlights and partially stocked starting bars are illustrative; glycogen is not visibly teal. High blood glucose reflects impaired regulation and insufficient insulin, not simply full storage tanks.

The heart is visible within the chest, with coronary arteries supplying its surface. The added surface fat is called epicardial fat; it grows with the visceral-fat example here. The baseline artwork already shows a small normal layer. This is an illustration of where fat can accumulate, not an additional diabetes threshold. Heart position and coronary connections follow OpenStax: Heart Anatomy.

The major arteries are shown through the tissues in a simplified front-view projection. Aortic-arch branches and limb pathways follow OpenStax: Circulatory Pathways. Glucose and lipid symbols are enlarged to show the changing concentration; they are not artery-blocking clots or plaque.

The estimated average glucose meter converts the illustrative HbA1c into the average glucose it corresponds to, using the ADAG equation: eAG (mg/dL) = 28.7 × HbA1c − 46.7 (mmol/L = mg/dL ÷ 18). HbA1c 5.7% ≈ 117 mg/dL and 6.5% ≈ 140 mg/dL mark the prediabetes and diabetes ranges. It is an average over about three months, not a single finger-stick or fasting reading. Source: Nathan et al., Diabetes Care 2008.

The plaque in the cut-open artery is illustrative. Atherosclerosis is driven by cholesterol-carrying particles (LDL and triglyceride-rich remnant particles) that enter the artery wall and are taken up by foam cells, first as flat fatty streaks and later as raised plaques with a lipid core under a fibrous cap. High glucose, insulin resistance and the high triglycerides of type 2 diabetes make this worse, which is why it appears here from the prediabetes range and is strongest in the diabetes range. The particles drawn are not to scale. When fats and glucose come back down, the simulator shrinks the plaque smoothly back to a clean wall so the direction of change is easy to see. Real plaque usually shrinks only partly and becomes more stable; it rarely disappears completely. Source: StatPearls: Atherosclerosis.

The personal fat threshold is a hypothesis about individual susceptibility to excess fat and type 2 diabetes. Fat under the skin (subcutaneous fat) can act as an energy reservoir. Limited healthy storage may contribute to fat accumulating around organs (visceral fat) and in tissues such as the liver and pancreas. The ReTUNE study supports the concept in people with type 2 diabetes at a lower BMI.

The numbered fat-storage marks organize the explanation; they are not established clinical thresholds or a universal sequence. Fat-depot shapes expand over one anatomical image. Added visceral tissue is rendered as one continuous abdominal fat bed behind a smaller organ core, while the outer abdominal fat layer moves outward. Organs and limbs are never stretched. Whole-body images do not crossfade. Every curve, body-shape change, and particle count is illustrative. Bar lengths compare a tissue with its own displayed range, not the quantity of fat in different tissues. A full bar means the end of this animation’s range, not a full organ.

Fat tissue can expand by enlarging existing cells (hypertrophy) and forming new cells from precursors (hyperplasia). Recruitment varies by fat depot; a human overfeeding study observed new fat-cell formation in lower-body fat. Extreme is a fictional high-capacity example, not a clinical category, and hyperplasia is not exclusive to it. Its controlled glucose does not imply unlimited safe storage: metabolically healthy obesity still carried increased future diabetes risk in the Whitehall II study. There is no validated “hundreds of pounds” cutoff in this model.

Insulin resistance and insulin-producing beta-cell function both matter. Extra circulating fats can appear before high blood glucose. Glucose rises because its regulation is impaired, not because it is physically squeezed out of fat stores. Muscle fat is not always harmful; trained athletes can store fat in muscle while remaining insulin sensitive. Fat loss can improve metabolic health, but moving this slider backward does not predict diabetes remission.

About HbA1c. The NIDDK A1C guide defines the prediabetes range as 5.7–6.4% and the diabetes range as 6.5% or above. HbA1c reflects average glucose over approximately 3 months. This model assigns fictional sustained-glucose examples to the slider; it cannot estimate a person's HbA1c, and moving it is not an immediate biological change. The number is rounded to one decimal and the label follows that displayed value. The illustrative starting value is 5.2%, a value inside the normal range, for every preset. While the pancreas compensates with more insulin, glucose drifts up only a little and stays in the normal range (to about 5.5%); it rises faster only late in the sequence, as it usually does in real life. The chosen endpoints are about 10.1% for Lower, 8.4% for Moderate, 7.0% for Larger, and 5.4% for Extreme; these illustrate contrasting scenarios, not typical outcomes for people with those body shapes. A diabetes diagnosis usually requires confirmation with another test.

Type 1 and type 2 have different mechanisms: see NIDDK: Symptoms & Causes of Diabetes.
