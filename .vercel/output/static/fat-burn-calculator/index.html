Maximal Fat Loss Calculator

This calculator shows the maximum fat-loss rate, designed to limit muscle loss as much as possible (no trial shows any rate spares muscle completely). You can choose any slower pace in between. One published model (Alpert, 2005) estimates that body fat can release at most about 31 kcal per pound of fat per day (range 29–34). This is an upper-limit estimate, not a proven ceiling. In that model, a calorie deficit bigger than this tends to come out of muscle and other lean tissue. Enter where you are and where you want to be, and this shows the fastest fat loss that still protects lean mass, week by week.

    How this is calculated

    The ceiling. Alpert (2005) analyzed underfeeding data, chiefly the Minnesota Starvation Experiment, and found that the fat store can transfer at most about 290 ± 25 kJ per kg of fat per day. That works out to 31.4 kcal per lb of fat per day. Ask for a bigger deficit than 31.4 × fat lb and the rest tends to come from lean tissue. Because the ceiling scales with how much fat you carry, it shrinks as you lean out.

    How solid is the 31? It is an upper-limit estimate from one theoretical analysis, not a measured physiological constant. The conversion is right (290 kJ/kg ÷ 4.184 ÷ 2.205 = 31.4 kcal/lb, and the paper's ±25 kJ gives 28.7–34.1). But the figure was fitted mostly to the Minnesota Starvation Experiment: lean young men, studied in the 1940s. It has not been confirmed in a prospective trial, and later starvation models treat it as one modelling assumption among several (Speakman & Westerterp, 2013). A claim online that Alpert later revised it to 20–25 kcal/lb was never published, so this calculator does not use it. In practice the limit matters most for lean people. With around 100 lb of fat or more, 31 × fat lb is about as large as a whole day's energy use, so the limit rarely binds. Use the range, not the single number.

    Fat over time. At the ceiling, fat mass decays exponentially: F(t) = F₀ · e^(−t / τ), where τ = ρ / k. k is the ceiling and ρ is the energy stored in a pound of fat mass, about 4,250 kcal/lb (≈ 9,400 kcal/kg, 39 MJ/kg). That makes τ ≈ 135 days, the same response time Alpert derived. Time to a goal is t = τ · ln(F₀ / F_goal) / pace.

    Why not 3,500 kcal per pound? The classic 3,500 kcal/lb rule (Wishnofsky, 1958) describes adipose tissue, which is only about 85–87% lipid. Body-fat % measures pure fat mass, which holds about 4,250 kcal/lb. Pairing Alpert's per-lb-of-fat-mass ceiling with 3,500 kcal/lb makes fat loss look about 20% faster than the data supports (τ = 113 days instead of 135). Hall (2008) also showed the 3,500 rule overstates long-term losses because it leaves out metabolic adaptation.

    Calorie targets (optional). BMR comes from Katch-McArdle (370 + 21.6 × lean kg) or Mifflin-St Jeor (1990). Either is multiplied by a standard activity factor to estimate TDEE. Target intake = TDEE − today's ceiling. Intake never drops below a floor of 1,500 kcal for men or 1,200 kcal for women, the usual minimum without medical supervision (NIH/NHLBI 1998 obesity guidelines). If the floor caps your deficit, the timeline slows to match.

    What it can't capture. Real bodies adapt: metabolic rate drops more than predicted (Hall et al., 2011, Lancet), water and glycogen swing the scale, and body-fat estimates from scales, calipers, or even DEXA can be off by 3–5 percentage points. Treat the result as a best case, and recheck your numbers every 4–6 weeks.
